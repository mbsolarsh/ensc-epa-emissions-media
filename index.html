Two Lanes, One Story

On February 12, 2026, the EPA got rid of the 2009 endangerment finding, which is basically the reason the government was allowed to limit greenhouse gases from cars and trucks. NPR and Fox News both covered it, and honestly, it's hard to believe they're talking about the same thing.

Topic: EPA vehicle emissions standards · Air Pollution & Climate Change

Let's go ↓

01 What actually happened?

1970

The Clean Air Act passes. It tells the EPA to regulate any "air pollution which may reasonably be anticipated to endanger public health or welfare."

2007

In Massachusetts v. EPA, the Supreme Court decides that greenhouse gases count as air pollution under the Clean Air Act.

2009

The EPA officially says six greenhouse gases (including CO2 and methane) endanger public health and welfare. That's the "endangerment finding," and it's what the limits on car and truck emissions were built on.

2024

The Biden administration sets the strictest car rules yet. The idea was that EVs could make up as much as 56% of new car sales by 2032.

Feb 2026

EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin gets rid of the endangerment finding along with every federal greenhouse gas rule for vehicles. Environmental groups immediately say they're going to sue.

How this connects to class: when you burn gas, you release CO2, a greenhouse gas that traps heat (Climate Change chapter). Tailpipes also give off NOx and particulates, which create smog and ground-level ozone (Air Pollution chapter). The thing that confused me at first is that this repeal is only about the greenhouse gas rules. The smog rules are separate and still in place, and neither article really explains that.

09 Artist's note

As a data science student, building interactive dashboards is honestly what I do best, so when I saw that Option 2 let me make any kind of visual, I knew I wanted to build a website instead of a poster. In my data classes we learn that a good dashboard doesn't just show people information, it lets them explore it. I wanted to bring that same idea to this assignment, because I think students (me included) remember things way better when we actually interact with them instead of just reading a wall of text. If you have to click, guess, or move a slider, you're paying attention.

That's why almost every section asks you to do something. The "Headline Lens" lets you flip between how NPR and Fox opened the same story, and that's where the difference hit me the most. NPR called it the administration's "most aggressive action yet" against climate efforts, while Fox said it was "saving Americans billions." The "Who Said It?" quiz makes you guess which outlet ran each quote, because guessing forces you to notice word choice. The flip cards in "What's Missing?" let you discover what each article left out on your own. I kept NPR blue and Fox red the whole way through so it's always clear which side you're looking at, and I designed it around a two-lane road since the topic is literally about cars. I even added the tailpipe calculator so you can plug in your own miles and see your car's CO2, because a number about yourself sticks more than a national statistic.

This project really deepened my understanding of vehicle emissions. I didn't realize that transportation is the biggest direct source of greenhouse gases in the U.S., or that one normal car puts out around 4.6 metric tons of CO2 a year. That one hit close to home because I actually own an electric car. Since it has no tailpipe, I never really thought about emissions at all, and I don't even have to get it emissions tested. Working on this made me realize that a lot of drivers are putting out tons of CO2 every year without thinking about it, and that my car isn't totally zero-emission either, since the electricity I charge it with still has to come from somewhere. It also made the EV part of the NPR article feel more personal, since the rules being rolled back were a big part of what was pushing automakers to make more cars like mine. I also learned that there are two different kinds of emissions rules. The endangerment finding was about greenhouse gases and climate change, but the pollutants that cause smog, like nitrogen oxides, are regulated separately. That made me understand why emissions testing matters. Those tests are what make sure cars on the road are actually meeting the standards, and without them the rules would just be on paper. Neither article explained this difference, which honestly surprised me. My biggest takeaway is that neither source gave me the full story by itself, and I only understood the issue after comparing both and looking at the actual data.

— Molly Solarsh, ENSC 1000, Fall 2026 ·
