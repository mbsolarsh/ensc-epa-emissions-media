Two Lanes, One Story

On February 12, 2026, the EPA repealed the 2009 endangerment finding, the scientific and legal basis for limiting greenhouse gas pollution from cars and trucks. NPR and Fox News both reported it. They told very different stories.

Topic: EPA vehicle emissions standards · Air Pollution & Climate Change

Start driving ↓

01 What actually happened?

1970

The Clean Air Act tells the EPA to regulate "air pollution which may reasonably be anticipated to endanger public health or welfare."

2007

Supreme Court, Massachusetts v. EPA: greenhouse gases count as air pollutants under the Clean Air Act.

2009

The EPA finds that six greenhouse gases (including CO2 and methane) endanger public health and welfare. This is the "endangerment finding." Tailpipe greenhouse gas limits for cars and trucks follow.

2024

Biden-era vehicle rules are the strictest yet. Officials expected EVs could reach up to 56% of new sales by 2032.

Feb 2026

EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin rescinds the endangerment finding and all federal greenhouse gas standards for vehicles. Environmental groups say they will sue.

Course link: burning gasoline releases CO2, a greenhouse gas that traps heat (Climate Change chapter). Tailpipes also release NOx and particulates that form smog and ground-level ozone (Air Pollution chapter). This repeal covers the greenhouse gas limits only. Separate rules for smog-forming pollutants still apply, and neither article spells out that difference clearly.

09 Artist's note

I built this as a website instead of a poster because I wanted the reader to do the comparison, not just read my conclusions. The main design idea is a two-lane road. Vehicle emissions are about cars, and the two articles travel side by side over the same facts while heading in different directions. NPR runs in a blue lane on the left and Fox News in a red lane on the right, because those are the colors most people already link to the left and right. I kept that color code everywhere on the site so the reader can always tell which source they are looking at. I chose two fonts: a bold, rounded font for headings, so the site feels energetic, and a serif font for the article text, so it reads like a newspaper.

Each section matches part of the assignment. The "Headline Lens" lets you switch between the two openings, and that is where the framing difference showed up most clearly for me. NPR's first sentence calls the repeal "its most aggressive action yet to halt initiatives that address planetary warming." Fox's first sentence says the change is "saving Americans billions." The "Who Said It?" quiz is there because guessing makes you notice word choice. Once you have seen "climate participation trophy" and "slap in the face," it is easy to tell the outlets apart. The emphasis meter and the "What's Missing?" cards show selection and omission. Fox spent a lot of space on the start-stop feature and didn't include climate science. NPR explained the science and the court fight, but its health coverage was thin, and the clearest health argument in either article actually came from Colorado Gov. Polis, quoted in Fox.

Making this changed how I understand the issue. Before, I thought "EPA vehicle standards" meant smog. I learned that the endangerment finding is specifically about greenhouse gases and climate change, and that rules for smog-forming pollutants are separate. Neither article explained that distinction, which shows how hard it is for readers to know exactly what changed. I also noticed that both outlets used the $1.3 trillion savings number, but only NPR questioned it by pointing out that drivers save on gasoline over time. The tailpipe calculator connects the topic to the course: one car's yearly CO2 turns into something you can see. I also tried to stay neutral. The action list has things anyone can do whatever their politics, like commenting on rules, voting, checking air quality, and reading more than one source. My biggest takeaway is that neither article is "the truth" by itself. I only got a full picture of the story by reading both.
